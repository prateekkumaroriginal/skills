name: choreography
description: Choose and time animations, transitions, and visual emphasis for motion-graphic videos. Use when storyboarding an explainer, animating a comparison, or revising a video whose motion obscures the point.
---

## Time the motion to the idea

For each beat, identify the phrase or action that cues it, the object the viewer should follow, and the frame they need time to read. With recorded narration, mark those cues in the actual audio. For silent video, use the appearance of a caption or a change in the depicted action. Start the movement as attention shifts. Let the result settle while the viewer still has time to connect it to the cue.

## Choose an animation

| What the viewer needs to understand | Motion to use |
| --- | --- |
| Several things belong to a group | Stagger a short rise and fade, following reading order. Reveal the whole group together when the point is its overall shape or size. |
| The same people or objects have a new arrangement | Move and resize the existing elements into their new positions. Keep an identifying feature visible throughout. Crossfade a heading if its wording changes. |
| Two things are connected | Draw a line between visible endpoints when the relationship is introduced. A dot traveling along the line can show direction or repeated flow. For a lasting association, the finished line is enough. |
| One detail in a dense source matters now | Establish the whole chart or diagram. Outline the relevant region, dim its surroundings, and connect the outline to a magnified inset. When attention shifts, move the outline to the next region. Keep the source, inset, units, and selected mark legible together. Redraw small labels at output resolution if the source crop looks soft. |
| A quantity builds toward a total | Grow a bar from its baseline. Drive its length and displayed number from the same progress value so they finish together. Stack segments only when their values add. Keep compared bars on a shared scale and label the units. Use a percentage fill only when the viewer can identify what 100% represents. |
| The viewer should compare several finished values | Keep rows, endpoints, and axes in place. Move the accent to the row being discussed and soften the others enough to preserve context. Pair color with a marker, outline, or label. Hold the completed comparison still. |

## Choose a transition

- **Stay in the shot** when only the focus changes. Fade a label or shift the highlight. Keep earlier evidence visible if the next claim depends on it.
- **Morph a shared object** when two layouts show successive states. Let the viewer track that object across the change.
- **Cut** when the thought changes cleanly or the next cue needs its full screen time. A fixed composition per idea works well for a narrated explainer. Use a pan or scroll when following a route, page, or other continuous space is itself the point.
- **Wipe** when a new section benefits from a visible boundary. Reuse one direction and easing pattern across equivalent section changes. The reference film used a left-to-right wipe with a thin accent line and a smooth start and stop over about 0.57 seconds. Adapt that timing to the cut and its typography.

## Check the result in playback

Watch a transition and a dense data beat at normal speed and intended viewing size. Can you follow the same object across the layout change? Is the active detail clear when its name is spoken? Can you read the final value and its units before the next beat? If two movements compete, delay the secondary one or leave that element still. If a label blurs during a zoom, inspect the encoded frame and fix the source resolution or render scale.
