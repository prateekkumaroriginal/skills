---
name: choreography
description: Use when creating, revising or reviewing a motion-graphic video — deciding what moves and how, how scenes transition, and where the viewer's attention goes.
---

Choreography is arranging, moving and transforming on-screen elements so each moment feels right. It has three parts: **animation** (how something behaves over time), **transition** (how the viewer gets from one scene or state to the next) and **focus** (where the viewer is looking right now). Let the moment set the pace and intensity: a small change gets a gentle shift, a surprise gets a sharp move, a detail worth reading gets stillness.

Examples below use generic objects (dot, card, grid, chart). They are a means to an end, not things to copy.

## Plan before animating

Write a **beat sheet** before any animation code. One row per beat, timed to the narration:

| Time | Narration keyword | Focal element | Motion | Camera | Everything else |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Every beat has exactly one focal element. If two things must be seen together, frame them as one group. The plan is done when every narration sentence maps to a beat and every beat has its focal element, motion, camera and treatment of the rest filled in.

## Animation

Motion tells the viewer what kind of thing they are watching. A pulsing dot marks a location, a moving dot shows direction, and the trail it leaves shows the route taken. Build a **motion vocabulary**: each kind of object gets its own verb, and it keeps that verb for the whole video.

- **Travel** (something going from A to B): an arc or a defined lane, ease-in-out, with a small settle on arrival. Duration scales with distance.
- **Entrance**: chosen by meaning. Grow from its source, draw on, slide in from where it came from, or split out of a parent. Ease-out, quick (250–500 ms).
- **Exit**: leaves toward where it goes or shrinks into what absorbs it. Ease-in, faster than the entrance.
- **Emphasis**: a brief scale pulse, a ring, a shake or a color flash, then back to rest.
- **State change** (whole becomes parts, box wraps content, card opens into detail): **morph** the one shape into the other so the viewer sees it is the same thing.

Add a short wind-up before important launches and follow-through on landings. Give contrasting objects contrasting motion: a request and its reply travel on different lanes, at different sizes or speeds.

A single fade-and-slide helper applied to everything is the signature of a flat video. Use a fade as one option among many, picked for a reason.

## Transitions

Choose a transition from how the next scene relates to the current one. Opening a card from a grid: expanding the card in place shows where the detail came from, sliding the grid aside makes the detail feel like the next page, and cutting straight to it gives the answer immediately. Same destination, different story.

| Relationship | Transition |
|---|---|
| Next scene is a detail of this one | Zoom or push into that detail |
| Next scene is the bigger picture | Pull back out |
| Next step in a sequence | Pan or slide in the direction of progress |
| Same objects, new arrangement | Shared elements move to their new places |
| Return to an earlier idea | Reverse of how you left it |
| Deliberate contrast or surprise | Hard cut |

Run every transition as a **relay**: one element carries attention across. The outgoing material leaves first, then the carried element moves, then new material arrives. Keep the overlap short (about 100 ms) so at most two layers are ever semi-transparent at once. Titles change after the scene settles, not as the transition itself.

## Focus

Ask: where on screen should the viewer be looking right now? Then make that the only answer. Use at least one of these on every beat, and stack them for the key moments:

- **Camera**: zoom or pan toward the focal element. Show a full calendar; on "these two days overlap," zoom until both entries fill the frame and stay readable.
- **Dim the rest**: lower everything not in play to about 35% opacity or desaturate it, and bring it back afterwards.
- **Scale**: the active element grows slightly (around 1.1×) while it speaks.
- **Progressive reveal**: show a chart up to a policy change; as the narrator explains what happened next, reveal the rest of the line left to right. Attention follows the new edge.
- **Build from blank**: on "a request arrives," one request appears; on "it waits," a queue forms; on "a worker picks it up," the worker appears and the request moves to it.

Color alone is a label, not focus. Give moving objects a **clear path**: nothing they cross or brush (titles, labels, other objects) competes with them, so move or fade whatever is in the way.

## Tempo

Vary the pace with the meaning. Routine exchanges are quick and snappy. The **climax** (the problem, the failure, the key insight) gets the tightest framing and a held moment of stillness before the resolution. The resolution gets a clear release, such as a settle, a pull back or a breath. Each entrance lands on its narration keyword, and each element stays until the sentence that needs it ends. While narration plays, keep the eye guided; a stretch of more than about 2.5 s with nothing changing needs a reason.

## Review

Score **Animation**, **Transition**, **Focus** and **Choreography** separately out of 10, from frames stepped through at 5–10 fps around every transition and key beat, not just stills. The review is done when every row of the beat sheet has been checked against the frames and each of these has an answer with timestamps:

- Do different kinds of objects move differently, or does everything share one motion?
- At each transition, which element carries attention, and how many semi-transparent layers overlap at the worst frame?
- Where does the camera move? A video with no zoom or pan anywhere needs a reason.
- At each beat, is there one obvious place to look? Note every frame where a moving object touches a title or label.
- Where is the climax, and is it staged differently from the routine beats?

Brief the reviewer with the video and this rubric only, not the list of recent fixes, so it judges the cut fresh.
